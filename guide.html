1 · Quick start

  • 🎓 Lesson (top right): a five-minute guided tour of the main idea. Each step sets up the map; stop anywhere and explore.
  • Time bar (top of the panel): drag through the model run, Now jumps to the present, ▶ plays (space bar; ← → step an hour, shift for 6 h).
  • Click any water: the point analyzer — that spot's tide and current over the whole run, with high/low water and slack marked and the gap between them labelled.
  • Colour: the flow right now (speed, flood/ebb, level, spin) or a pattern over the run (the phase map and nine more).
  • 📋 What happens here?: the current area described in words, from this run.
  • Mouse: drag to rotate, right-drag (or shift-drag) to pan, wheel to zoom. 2D (key 2) gives a north-up map. Keys 1, 3–9 jump to views.

2 · The question, and the answer

A tide table gives the times of high and low water; a current table gives the times of slack water and the strongest flood and ebb. In much of Puget Sound the two line up: slack comes close to high and low water. In the Strait of Juan de Fuca they don't: the current runs hardest near high and low water and turns around half-tide. At Deception Pass slack can come an hour or two from high water on either side. Why?

A tide is a wave, and a wave can travel or slosh

The tide enters from the Pacific as a very long wave (hundreds of kilometres from crest to crest).

Where it travels (a progressive wave) the water under the crest moves forward with it and the water under the trough moves back: the current is in step with the level — strongest at high and low water, slack in between, about three hours (a quarter of the 12.4-hour tide) from high or low water.

Where it reflects off the head of a basin, the incoming and reflected waves add up to a standing wave: the whole basin rises and falls together. Then the current is set by how fast the basin fills — water must flow in to raise the level, so the current is strongest when the level rises fastest (half-tide) and stops when the level stops rising (high water). Slack comes at high and low water.

In the Salish Sea: the twice-daily tide behaves largely as a progressive wave in Juan de Fuca and excites standing waves in the Strait of Georgia and Puget Sound (Mofjeld & Larsen 1984; Foreman et al. 1995). The Strait of Georgia sees high water within about ten minutes end to end; Puget Sound within about 40 minutes (a ~20° phase change), with a ~40° jump across Deception Pass. This model's phase map shows the same: Juan de Fuca red (slack ~3 h from high/low water), Puget Sound and the Gulf Islands blue, and the change happening across the San Juans and Haro and Rosario straits. In this model, Admiralty Inlet is already on the standing side (slack within about half an hour of high and low water); the lesson and the maps say so rather than repeating the common shorthand that Admiralty Inlet is the transition.

Water in = basin rising

The cleanest proof is Basins & passes: the water flowing through Tacoma Narrows and the rate South Sound fills (its area × how fast its level rises) are computed independently from the model, and they are the same curve (correlation 0.998 in the first run). The flow stops when the level stops rising: slack at high water.

Passes between two tides

A short pass between two basins whose tides differ (Deception Pass) is driven by the difference in level across it: the water runs downhill, and turns when the levels cross — not at high water on either side.

The mixed tide

The Salish Sea's tide is mixed: a twice-daily part (M2) and a daily part (K1) of similar size. One of the day's two high waters is often much higher than the other, and sometimes one barely happens. Slack times then shift from tide to tide. The phase map uses the twice-daily tide alone (stable); the point analyzer shows the actual events (what you would meet).

Compare the tides (lab runs)

Spring tides come near new and full moon, when the sun's and moon's pulls line up; neap tides at the quarter moons, when they pull at right angles (NOAA Ocean Service). With the spring and neap lab runs published, two maps compare them (on any lab run, and on Live):

One thing works the other way round: the slow two-layer exchange at Admiralty Inlet is strongest at neaps. Over each run, the deep inflow at the section's deepest point averages 0.28 kt at the neap against 0.01 kt at the spring — weaker tides stir the layers less over the sill, so the dense water flows in underneath more freely. Geyer & Cannon (1982) observed the same fortnightly swing there.

Typically 10–15 minutes; the most, 10–30 minutes later at springs, at the east end of Juan de Fuca and in Haro Strait. The size of the current changes with the tide's range; its timing hardly does.

5 · The point analyzer

Click any water. The card shows the whole run at that spot: the water level (top) with each high and low water timed, and the current along the local flood direction (bottom; flood blue above zero, ebb orange below), with slack (purple) and the strongest flood and ebb marked. Dashed lines join each slack to the nearest high or low water and label the gap (±h:mm). Hover for values; click to move the time there. The summary gives the median slack offsets in this run, the twice-daily timing from the phase map, and the tidal current and range of the twice-daily and daily tides.

Times come from hourly model output: high and low water by a parabola through the three hourly values around each, slack by linear interpolation of the zero crossing, peak currents by parabola. That gives minutes, not hours, but treat them as ±10 min.

6 · Basins & passes

For South Sound (through Tacoma Narrows), Hood Canal (through its mouth) and all of Puget Sound (through Admiralty Inlet, Deception Pass and the Swinomish Channel), the pipeline adds up, every hour, the water flowing through lines drawn across the passes (depth-averaged velocity across the line × water depth × length), and separately the basin's wet area × the rate its area-mean level rises. The basin is everything on the model's mesh reachable from inside without crossing the pass lines. If the model conserves water (it does; rivers add a little), the two match — they do, r ≥ 0.998. The card states the peak flows, the water each flood brings in (the tidal prism) and its share of the basin's volume, and, for Puget Sound, how the exchange divides between the passes.

Deception Pass has its own card: the level on the Rosario Strait side and the Skagit Bay side, and the current through the pass.

The model's mesh joins Oakland Bay (Shelton) to Hood Canal through a shallow marsh channel (under 1 m deep). That link is closed for the basin calculation; its peak flow (~100 m³/s against ~100,000 m³/s through the Narrows) is reported by the pipeline.

7 · Area reports

“📋 What happens here?” writes up the area of the current view (or the whole inland sea) from this run's patterns: the tide's range and how high water arrives; the strength and direction of the current and its fastest spot; slack timing; daily vs twice-daily, rotary, flood/ebb dominance; eddies; net drift and layering. Each statement has a “map ›” link to its colour map and, where there's a hot spot, flies there. Places are named from a short list of landmarks with approximate positions (to ~1 km).

9 · How it's computed

Packing the run

Every hour: water level at each node (centimetres; drying nodes flagged), and the surface current at each triangle (east and north, 0.1 kt steps; the few values beyond 12.7 kt — Seymour Narrows runs near 16 kt at springs — are kept exactly in an overflow list) — about 15 MB a run. Lab runs add the depth-averaged current (24 MB a run) and the full volume: all ten layers of east, north and vertical velocity, ~170 MB on request.

Smooth on screen

Triangle values are carried to the corners as area-weighted averages, so colours are smooth across the mesh; time is blended linearly between hours on the graphics card; depth slices interpolate between layer centres using each triangle's depth plus the water level.

Particles and streamlines

The current is sampled onto a regular grid for them: 250 m over the whole region, and, once you zoom in under about 30 km across and stop, a finer grid of just the view at about 1/250 of its width (down to 40 m) — finer than the model's own triangles (~75 m in the tightest passes), so the particles follow every eddy the model resolves. The model itself resolves eddies down to roughly half a kilometre to a kilometre (a few triangles across) and saves hourly; anything smaller or faster isn't in the data.

Curtains

Each pass line is sampled every 60 m where it crosses the model's water; each column takes the ten layer values of its triangle at the layer centres (σ × the local depth), and the current across the line. Which side is “into the basin” is taken from the basin's flow: the depth-averaged current across the line must rise and fall with the pass's published flux. The card's average by depth is the plain mean over the run's 73 hours.

Flood direction

Each triangle's flood direction is its principal axis of depth-averaged flow over the run, pointed the way the water goes while the local tide is rising or high: the twice-daily current phase must lie within 90° of (water-level phase − 45°), between “strongest at mid-rise” (standing) and “strongest at high water” (progressive).

The phase map

Least-squares fit of mean + trend + M2 + K1 + M4 to each triangle's water level and along-axis depth-averaged current over the 73 hours. Slack after high water = (current phase + 90° − level phase) mod 180°, in hours. Seventy-two hours separates M2, K1 and M4 but not their neighbours (S2 and N2 fold into M2, O1 into K1), so the map is a snapshot of this run's twice-daily behaviour; it's stable from run to run but shifts a little between spring and neap tides.

Patterns

Arrival times are the same fit's M2 phases, measured from M2 high water at the triangle nearest the ocean entrance off Neah Bay. Vorticity and the Okubo–Weiss parameter come from the gradients of the surface current across each triangle.

10 · Limits and caveats

  • It's a model forecast. SSCOFS is NOAA's operational model and is checked against tide gauges and current meters, but it is not observation. Wind, rivers and weather in the forecast affect levels and surface currents.
  • Water level datum is the model's own (its files say “above geoid”), not chart datum (MLLW). Checked against NOAA's predictions at Seattle, Port Townsend, Friday Harbor and Tacoma over three runs: the model's level sits a steady 0.34–0.45 m above NOAA's mean sea level, its range is within about 5 % of NOAA's and its high and low waters come within the hour (about 5–10 minutes at Seattle). At all 141 NOAA tide stations the range is within about ±7 % at springs (±15 % at neaps) and high and low water within about 20 minutes at springs — the tide itself is well modelled everywhere; it's the currents in the narrowest passes that aren't. Tide tables use MLLW, 1.4–2.1 m below mean sea level here; near a NOAA tide station the point panel shows NOAA's own heights. Good for range and timing, not for depth under the keel.
  • Hourly output. Event times are interpolated; expect about ±10 minutes.
  • Seventy-two hours can't separate all the tidal constituents: the phase map is a snapshot of one run, and the net exchange is shown only where the run is long enough to trust. Spring–neap changes are covered by the three lab runs (spring, between, neap).
  • Deception Pass: the model's slack times at the narrows agree with NOAA's predictions to about 10 minutes (median, spring and in-between runs; noisier at neaps), but its speeds are about half NOAA's — see “Where the model is weak” below. Slack there comes well before high or low water at the pass itself, because the current is driven by the level difference across the pass.
  • “Flood” direction in short passes. Each place's flood direction comes from its own tide timing (section 9). At 5 of 154 NOAA current stations that rule points the other way from NOAA's; two are in Deception Pass, where the “Flood or ebb” colours therefore take NOAA's sense (flood east, into Skagit Bay) within 4 km of its stations. Near any NOAA station the point panel measures the current along NOAA's own flood direction.
  • The “surface” current is the model's top layer: the top ~3 % of the depth (about 1 m in 30 m of water, 6 m in 200 m).
  • Seabed: survey depths (NOAA CUDEM in US waters, CHS NONNA in Canadian waters) resampled to 250 m, blended over 3 km where sources meet; the open coast and shelf show the model's own depth. Choose “Model's own” under the seabed settings to see what the model's water actually flows over — the difference shows where its mesh smooths sills and channels.
  • Where the model is weak — narrow passes. Checked against NOAA's current predictions at 108 stations (1–4 Oct 2026): at most the model's peak currents are within about 15 % of NOAA's (median 1.06×), but in constrictions narrower than its ~100 m triangles can resolve it runs far too slow — Deception Pass narrows: ebbs of 2.5–2.8 kt where NOAA predicts 6.0–7.9 kt, floods ~60 %; also Saddle Bag Island Passage, Totten Inlet entrance, Pickering Passage, Drayton Harbor entrance (⅓–½), Guemes Channel and Thatcher Pass (~⅔). It runs too fast in a few open spots (south of Discovery Island, Harbor Island, ~2×). Each run is rated again station by station; the map marks the stations where that run is far off (orange: too weak, violet: too strong), and clicking one shows NOAA's curve beside the model's. In those passes, use NOAA's predictions for speeds.
  • NOAA's station predictions are tidal only (no wind or river flow) and at a set depth; stored with the site a year at a time and drawn between their slack and peak times with NOAA's usual sine curve.
